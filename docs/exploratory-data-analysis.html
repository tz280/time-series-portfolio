Food Inflation and the Cost of Living: An Essential Connection

Food represents the most immediate and unavoidable component of household cost of living. Unlike discretionary expenses that can be deferred or eliminated, food consumption is fixed—families must eat regardless of price levels. This makes food inflation uniquely impactful on household budgets, particularly for lower-income families who allocate 15-20% of their income to food compared to 10-12% for higher-income households.

The 2010-2024 period captured in this analysis spans a fundamental transition in American food affordability. What began as a decade of predictable, manageable price increases transformed into the most rapid food cost acceleration in forty years. For households operating on fixed or slowly-growing incomes, this wasn’t an abstract economic statistic—it was the difference between filling a grocery cart for $100 versus $130, between weekly restaurant meals becoming monthly luxuries, between comfortable budgeting and constant financial stress.

The time series analysis that follows dissects this transformation through multiple lenses: trend identification reveals the structural break at COVID-19; autocorrelation analysis confirms price increases are permanent rather than reversible; decomposition shows trend dominance over seasonality; and stationarity tests prove each inflation shock resets the baseline permanently higher. Together, these technical findings translate to a simple household reality: food costs won’t return to pre-pandemic levels, budget adjustments must be permanent, and the middle-class consumption patterns of 2019 now require upper-middle-class incomes in 2024.

# Load data function
load_fred_csv <- function(file_path, series_name) {

  tryCatch({
    data <- read_csv(file_path, show_col_types = FALSE)
    
    # Handle different CSV formats
    col_names <- names(data)
    date_col <- col_names[grepl("DATE|date|Date", col_names)][1]
    if (is.na(date_col)) date_col <- col_names[1]
    value_col <- col_names[!col_names %in% date_col][1]
    
    data <- data %>% 
      select(date = all_of(date_col), value = all_of(value_col)) %>%
      mutate(
        date = as.Date(date),
        value = as.numeric(as.character(value))
      ) %>%
      filter(!is.na(date), !is.na(value)) %>%
      arrange(date)
    
    return(data)
    
  }, error = function(e) {
    cat("✗ Error loading", series_name, ":", e$message, "\n")
    return(NULL)
  })
}

# Create time series objects
create_ts_objects <- function(data) {
  available_cols <- names(data)[names(data) != "date"]
  ts_list <- list()
  
  # Determine start date and frequency
  start_year <- year(min(data$date))
  start_month <- month(min(data$date))
  
  for (col in available_cols) {
    ts_list[[col]] <- ts(data[[col]], start = c(start_year, start_month), frequency = 12)
  }
  
  return(ts_list)
}

ts_data <- create_ts_objects(combined_food_data)

These time series demonstrate a clear structural break at the COVID-19 pandemic. Pre-2020 food prices followed stable, predictable growth patterns of roughly 2-3% annually. Post-2020, all categories accelerated sharply—groceries increased 30% in four years, equivalent to a decade of pre-pandemic inflation compressed into half the time.

The “Food Away from Home” series shows the steepest and most persistent acceleration, driven by labor market pressures in service industries. Lower-income households, which allocate larger budget shares to food, face the greatest pressure with the least flexibility to adjust. This created sustained real income erosion that required both household behavioral adjustment and monetary policy intervention to address.

All three CPI series exhibit extremely strong positive autocorrelation across all lag periods, as evidenced by the tight clustering of points along the diagonal reference lines. This pattern reveals several key insights:

The high persistence at lag 1 confirms that food prices are highly predictable in the short term—this month’s prices closely predict next month’s. From a household perspective, this strong autocorrelation means food price increases are highly predictable but non-reversible. Unlike energy prices that can spike and retreat, food inflation exhibits a ratchet effect—prices rise and stay elevated. This creates sustained purchasing power erosion rather than temporary shocks, making budget adjustment particularly challenging for households.

The multiplicative decomposition reveals that food CPI series are overwhelmingly trend-driven with minimal seasonal or random components. The seasonal fluctuations oscillate within an extremely narrow band (±0.1% around 1.0), indicating food prices exhibit virtually no calendar-based patterns. This contradicts common assumptions about harvest cycles affecting food costs—in reality, modern supply chains and processed food dominance smooth out agricultural seasonality.

The trend component tells the critical story. “Food at Home” shows a decade of stability (2010-2020) followed by sharp acceleration, while “Food Away from Home” demonstrates relentless linear growth throughout. The bend in the trend curves around 2020-2021 quantifies the regime shift: the slope literally doubles, representing a structural change in inflation dynamics rather than cyclical variation.

The remainder component spikes dramatically around 2020-2021 across all three series. These are shocks that the model couldn’t explain through trend or seasonality—supply chain disruptions, pandemic-related demand shifts, and policy interventions. The fact that these “unexplained” deviations remain elevated post-2021 suggests the model struggles with the new inflation regime. What decomposition treats as random error is actually persistent structural change.

For households, the absence of seasonality means no timing strategies exist for reducing food costs. Unlike energy bills that might be reduced by seasonal timing, food inflation is monotonic and unavoidable. The trend dominance confirms that food price increases are systematic and permanent, not temporary fluctuations households can wait out. The remainder spikes show how external shocks create step-changes in the price level that never reverse—each crisis ratchets costs higher without subsequent relief.

# ACF AND PACF ANALYSIS

create_acf_pacf_analysis <- function(ts_obj, series_name, max_lag = 36) {
  
  # Calculate ACF and PACF
  acf_result <- acf(ts_obj, lag.max = max_lag, plot = FALSE)
  pacf_result <- pacf(ts_obj, lag.max = max_lag, plot = FALSE)
  
  # Create ACF data frame
  acf_df <- data.frame(
    lag = acf_result$lag[,,1],
    acf = acf_result$acf[,,1],
    type = "ACF (Autocorrelation Function)"
  )
  
  # Create PACF data frame  
  pacf_df <- data.frame(
    lag = pacf_result$lag[,,1],
    acf = pacf_result$acf[,,1],
    type = "PACF (Partial Autocorrelation Function)"
  )
  
  # Combine data
  combined_df <- rbind(acf_df, pacf_df)
  
  # Calculate confidence intervals
  n <- length(ts_obj)
  ci <- qnorm(0.975) / sqrt(n)
  
  p <- ggplot(combined_df, aes(x = lag, y = acf)) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 0, color = "black", size = 0.5) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = c(ci, -ci), color = "blue", linetype = "dashed", alpha = 0.7) +
    geom_segment(aes(xend = lag, yend = 0), color = "#1f77b4", size = 1) +
    geom_point(color = "#1f77b4", size = 2) +
    facet_wrap(~ type, ncol = 1, scales = "free_y") +
    labs(
      title = paste("Autocorrelation Analysis:", series_name),
      subtitle = "ACF and PACF plots for stationarity and model identification",
      x = "Lag",
      y = "Autocorrelation",
      caption = "Dashed blue lines: 95% confidence intervals | Values outside indicate significant correlation"
    ) +
    theme_minimal() +
    theme(
      strip.background = element_rect(fill = "lightgray", alpha = 0.5),
      strip.text = element_text(face = "bold", size = 11),
      plot.title = element_text(size = 16, face = "bold")
    ) +
    scale_x_continuous(breaks = seq(0, max_lag, by = 6))
  
  return(list(plot = p, acf_values = acf_result, pacf_values = pacf_result))
}

All three series display classic non-stationary characteristics. The ACF shows extremely slow, linear decay rather than rapid exponential decline—autocorrelations remain above 0.5 even at lag 20+. This confirms food prices contain a strong trend component with no mean reversion. The PACF cuts off sharply after lag 1, suggesting an autoregressive process of order 1 with an integrated component (likely ARIMA(1,1,0) or similar). The absence of seasonal spikes at lag 12 in both ACF and PACF is notable—no distinctive patterns appear at annual intervals. This reinforces that food inflation operates independently of agricultural cycles or holiday demand patterns. The pricing mechanisms reflect supply chain intermediation and retailer strategies rather than raw commodity seasonality.

“Food Away from Home” shows the strongest persistence—its ACF decays even more slowly than groceries, and autocorrelations remain significant across all lags shown. This reflects sticky menu pricing in restaurants where costs accumulate over time but price adjustments occur infrequently in large discrete jumps rather than continuous small changes.

For households, this persistence pattern means food price shocks have permanent effects. Unlike stationary processes where deviations eventually revert to mean, these series exhibit unit root behavior—each price increase becomes the new baseline. The high lag-1 autocorrelation (>0.95) indicates prices essentially follow a random walk with drift, making future costs predictable in direction (upward) but not magnitude. Families cannot budget based on historical averages because those averages become obsolete after each inflationary shock.

# AUGMENTED DICKEY-FULLER STATIONARITY TESTS

perform_adf_tests <- function(ts_data, series_names) {
  
  adf_results <- data.frame(
    Series = character(),
    ADF_Statistic = numeric(),
    P_Value = numeric(),
    Critical_Value_1pct = numeric(),
    Critical_Value_5pct = numeric(),
    Critical_Value_10pct = numeric(),
    Is_Stationary_5pct = logical(),
    Is_Stationary_1pct = logical(),
    stringsAsFactors = FALSE
  )
  
  cat("Performing ADF tests...\n")
  
  for(i in 1:length(series_names)) {
    series_name <- series_names[i]
    ts_obj <- ts_data[[series_name]]
    
    # Clean data more thoroughly
    ts_numeric <- as.numeric(ts_obj)  # Convert to numeric first
    ts_clean <- ts_numeric[!is.na(ts_numeric) & is.finite(ts_numeric)]  # Remove NA and infinite values
    
    # Check if we have enough data points
    if (length(ts_clean) < 10) {
      cat("✗ Insufficient data for", series_name, "(", length(ts_clean), "valid points)\n")
      next
    }
    
    # Perform ADF test
    tryCatch({
      adf_test <- adf.test(ts_clean, alternative = "stationary")
      
      display_name <- case_when(
        series_name == "food_at_home" ~ "Food at Home CPI",
        series_name == "food_away_home" ~ "Food Away from Home CPI",
        series_name == "cpi_all" ~ "Overall CPI (All Items)",
        TRUE ~ str_to_title(str_replace_all(series_name, "_", " "))
      )
      
      # Extract critical values properly
      critical_values <- adf_test$parameter
      if (is.null(critical_values) || length(critical_values) == 0) {
        # Use standard critical values if not available
        crit_1pct <- -3.43
        crit_5pct <- -2.86
        crit_10pct <- -2.57
      } else {
        crit_1pct <- ifelse(length(critical_values) >= 1, critical_values[1], -3.43)
        crit_5pct <- ifelse(length(critical_values) >= 2, critical_values[2], -2.86)
        crit_10pct <- ifelse(length(critical_values) >= 3, critical_values[3], -2.57)
      }
      
      adf_results <<- rbind(adf_results, data.frame(
        Series = display_name,
        ADF_Statistic = round(adf_test$statistic, 4),
        P_Value = round(adf_test$p.value, 4),
        Critical_Value_1pct = round(crit_1pct, 4),
        Critical_Value_5pct = round(crit_5pct, 4), 
        Critical_Value_10pct = round(crit_10pct, 4),
        Is_Stationary_5pct = adf_test$p.value < 0.05,
        Is_Stationary_1pct = adf_test$p.value < 0.01
      ))
      
      cat("✓", display_name, "- ADF Statistic:", round(adf_test$statistic, 4), 
          ", p-value:", round(adf_test$p.value, 4), 
          ", Stationary (5%):", adf_test$p.value < 0.05, "\n")
      
    }, error = function(e) {
      cat("✗ Error testing", series_name, ":", e$message, "\n")
      
      # Print diagnostic info
      cat("   Data type:", class(ts_clean), "\n")
      cat("   Data length:", length(ts_clean), "\n")
      cat("   Data range:", paste(round(range(ts_clean, na.rm = TRUE), 2), collapse = " to "), "\n")
      cat("   First few values:", paste(head(ts_clean, 5), collapse = ", "), "\n")
    })
  }
  
  return(adf_results)
}

# MAKING SERIES STATIONARY 
make_stationary <- function(ts_obj, series_name) {
  
  display_name <- case_when(
    series_name == "food_at_home" ~ "Food at Home CPI",
    series_name == "food_away_home" ~ "Food Away from Home CPI", 
    series_name == "cpi_all" ~ "Overall CPI (All Items)",
    TRUE ~ str_to_title(str_replace_all(series_name, "_", " "))
  )

  # First differencing
  diff1 <- diff(ts_obj, differences = 1)
  
  # Test stationarity of differenced series
  adf_test_diff1 <- adf.test(diff1[!is.na(diff1)], alternative = "stationary")
  
  # Create comparison plots - Original vs First Difference
  original_df <- data.frame(
    date = as.Date(time(ts_obj)),
    value = as.numeric(ts_obj),
    series_type = "Original Series"
  )
  
  diff1_df <- data.frame(
    date = as.Date(time(diff1)),
    value = as.numeric(diff1),
    series_type = "First Difference"
  )
  
  combined_df <- rbind(original_df, diff1_df)
  
  p1 <- ggplot(combined_df, aes(x = date, y = value)) +
    geom_line(color = "#1f77b4", size = 1) +
    facet_wrap(~ series_type, scales = "free_y", ncol = 1) +
    labs(
      title = paste("Stationarity Transformation:", display_name),
      subtitle = paste("First difference ADF p-value:", round(adf_test_diff1$p.value, 4)),
      x = "Date",
      y = "Value",
      caption = "First differencing removes trend to achieve stationarity"
    ) +
    theme_minimal() +
    theme(
      strip.background = element_rect(fill = "lightblue", alpha = 0.3),
      strip.text = element_text(face = "bold", size = 12),
      plot.title = element_text(size = 16, face = "bold")
    )
  
  # ACF comparison - Original vs Differenced
  acf_orig <- acf(ts_obj[!is.na(ts_obj)], lag.max = 24, plot = FALSE)
  acf_diff1 <- acf(diff1[!is.na(diff1)], lag.max = 24, plot = FALSE)
  
  acf_orig_df <- data.frame(
    lag = acf_orig$lag[,,1],
    acf = acf_orig$acf[,,1],
    series_type = "Original Series ACF"
  )
  
  acf_diff1_df <- data.frame(
    lag = acf_diff1$lag[,,1],
    acf = acf_diff1$acf[,,1], 
    series_type = "First Difference ACF"
  )
  
  acf_combined <- rbind(acf_orig_df, acf_diff1_df)
  
  n <- length(ts_obj[!is.na(ts_obj)])
  ci <- qnorm(0.975) / sqrt(n)
  
  p2 <- ggplot(acf_combined, aes(x = lag, y = acf)) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 0, color = "black") +
    geom_hline(yintercept = c(ci, -ci), color = "blue", linetype = "dashed", alpha = 0.7) +
    geom_segment(aes(xend = lag, yend = 0), color = "#1f77b4", size = 1) +
    geom_point(color = "#1f77b4", size = 2) +
    facet_wrap(~ series_type, ncol = 1) +
    labs(
      title = paste("ACF Comparison after Differencing:", display_name),
      subtitle = "Comparing autocorrelation structure before and after first differencing",
      x = "Lag",

# LOG TRANSFORMATION 

check_heteroscedasticity <- function(ts_obj, series_name) {
  
  display_name <- case_when(
    series_name == "food_at_home" ~ "Food at Home CPI",
    series_name == "food_away_home" ~ "Food Away from Home CPI",
    series_name == "cpi_all" ~ "Overall CPI (All Items)",
    TRUE ~ str_to_title(str_replace_all(series_name, "_", " "))
  )
  
  cat("Checking heteroscedasticity for", display_name, "\n")
  
  # Original series
  original_df <- data.frame(
    date = as.Date(time(ts_obj)),
    value = as.numeric(ts_obj),
    log_value = log(as.numeric(ts_obj))
  )
  
  # Calculate residuals from trend
  original_df$trend <- as.numeric(fitted(loess(value ~ as.numeric(date), data = original_df)))
  original_df$residuals <- original_df$value - original_df$trend
  
  # Log residuals
  original_df$log_trend <- as.numeric(fitted(loess(log_value ~ as.numeric(date), data = original_df)))
  original_df$log_residuals <- original_df$log_value - original_df$log_trend
  
  # Create comparison plots
  p1 <- original_df %>%
    select(date, value, log_value) %>%
    pivot_longer(cols = c(value, log_value), names_to = "transformation", values_to = "val") %>%
    mutate(
      transformation_clean = case_when(
        transformation == "value" ~ "Original Series",
        transformation == "log_value" ~ "Log-Transformed Series"
      )
    ) %>%
    ggplot(aes(x = date, y = val)) +
    geom_line(color = "#1f77b4", size = 1) +
    facet_wrap(~ transformation_clean, scales = "free_y", ncol = 1) +
    labs(
      title = paste("Original vs Log-Transformed Series:", display_name),
      subtitle = "Log transformation can stabilize variance if heteroscedasticity is present",
      x = "Date",
      y = "Value",
      caption = "Log transformation reduces exponential growth patterns"
    ) +
    theme_minimal() +
    theme(
      strip.background = element_rect(fill = "lightgreen", alpha = 0.3),
      strip.text = element_text(face = "bold", size = 11),
      plot.title = element_text(size = 16, face = "bold")
    )
  
  # Residuals comparison
  p2 <- original_df %>%
    select(date, residuals, log_residuals) %>%
    pivot_longer(cols = c(residuals, log_residuals), names_to = "residual_type", values_to = "residual") %>%
    mutate(
      residual_type_clean = case_when(
        residual_type == "residuals" ~ "Original Residuals",
        residual_type == "log_residuals" ~ "Log-Transformed Residuals"
      )
    ) %>%
    ggplot(aes(x = date, y = residual)) +
    geom_line(color = "#d62728", size = 0.8) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", color = "gray50") +
    facet_wrap(~ residual_type_clean, scales = "free_y", ncol = 1) +
    labs(
      title = paste("Residuals Comparison:", display_name),
      subtitle = "Comparing variance stability between original and log-transformed residuals",
      x = "Date",
      y = "Residuals",
      caption = "More stable residuals (constant variance) suggest appropriate transformation"
    ) +
    theme_minimal() +
    theme(
      strip.background = element_rect(fill = "lightcoral", alpha = 0.3),
      strip.text = element_text(face = "bold", size = 11)
    )
  
  # Calculate variance statistics
  orig_var <- var(original_df$residuals, na.rm = TRUE)
  log_var <- var(original_df$log_residuals, na.rm = TRUE)
  
  cat("  Original residuals variance:", round(orig_var, 4), "\n")
  cat("  Log-transformed residuals variance:", round(log_var, 4), "\n")
  cat("  Variance ratio (log/original):", round(log_var/orig_var, 4), "\n")
  
  return(list(
    comparison_plot = p1,
    residuals_plot = p2,
    log_ts = ts(original_df$log_value, start = start(ts_obj), frequency = frequency(ts_obj)),
    variance_improvement = log_var < orig_var
  ))
}

Interest Rates and Household Borrowing Costs: The Hidden Inflation Multiplier

Interest rates represent the price of money itself—the cost households pay to bridge the gap between current income and major life purchases. Unlike food or energy prices that directly extract dollars from monthly budgets, interest rates determine whether families can access homeownership, vehicle ownership, and higher education at all. A mortgage rate increase from 3% to 7% doesn’t just raise monthly payments—it prices millions out of the housing market entirely, forcing them into rental markets where they face the combined burden of rising rents plus inability to build home equity.

The 2020-2024 period captured an unprecedented interest rate whipsaw: emergency accommodation drove rates to historic lows, creating a brief window for refinancing and purchases, followed by the fastest tightening cycle in four decades. This volatility transformed interest rates from a stable background parameter families could plan around into an unpredictable variable that invalidated multi-year financial strategies within months. The time series analysis reveals how policy-driven rate regimes exhibit high persistence within periods but abrupt transitions between them—creating a binary environment where timing luck matters more than prudent planning, and where yesterday’s 3% mortgage becomes today’s unobtainable dream while housing prices refuse to adjust downward.

ma_windows <- c(3, 6, 12, 24, 36)

calculate_ma <- function(ts_data, window) {
  ma_result <- stats::filter(ts_data, rep(1/window, window), sides = 2)
  return(ma_result)
}

treasury_ma <- lapply(ma_windows, function(w) calculate_ma(treasury_ts, w))
names(treasury_ma) <- paste("MA", ma_windows, sep = "_")

fed_ma <- lapply(ma_windows, function(w) calculate_ma(fed_ts, w))
names(fed_ma) <- paste("MA", ma_windows, sep = "_")

create_ma_plot <- function(original_ts, ma_list, title_prefix) {
  time_index <- time(original_ts)
  
  plot_data <- data.frame(
    time = as.numeric(time_index),
    original = as.numeric(original_ts)
  )
  
  for(i in 1:length(ma_list)) {
    plot_data[[names(ma_list)[i]]] <- as.numeric(ma_list[[i]])
  }
  
  p <- ggplot(plot_data, aes(x = time)) +
    geom_line(aes(y = original, color = "Original"), alpha = 0.7) +
    geom_line(aes(y = MA_3, color = "MA(3)"), size = 0.8) +
    geom_line(aes(y = MA_6, color = "MA(6)"), size = 0.8) +
    geom_line(aes(y = MA_12, color = "MA(12)"), size = 1) +
    geom_line(aes(y = MA_24, color = "MA(24)"), size = 1) +
    geom_line(aes(y = MA_36, color = "MA(36)"), size = 1.2) +
    scale_color_manual(values = c(
      "Original" = "gray70",
      "MA(3)" = "#E74C3C",
      "MA(6)" = "#F39C12", 
      "MA(12)" = "#3498DB",
      "MA(24)" = "#2ECC71",
      "MA(36)" = "#9B59B6"
    )) +
    labs(title = paste(title_prefix, "- Moving Average Smoothing"),
         x = "Year",
         y = "Interest Rate (%)",
         color = "Series") +
    theme_minimal() +
    theme(legend.position = "bottom")
  
  return(p)
}

treasury_ma_plot <- create_ma_plot(treasury_ts, treasury_ma, "10-Year Treasury Rate")
fed_ma_plot <- create_ma_plot(fed_ts, fed_ma, "Federal Funds Rate")

10-Year Treasury: All MA windows converge closely during 2010-2019, confirming stable rate environment. Post-2020 shows dramatic divergence—short MAs (3, 6) capture the rapid collapse to 0.7% and subsequent spike to 4.5%, while MA36 lags significantly. By 2024, MAs reconverge around 4%, but the gap during 2020-2023 quantifies the unprecedented volatility households faced.

Federal Funds Rate: The staircase pattern remains visible even in long MAs. MA36 completely smooths out the 2016-2019 gradual rise, showing only the zero-rate plateaus (2010-2015, 2020-2021) and the 2022-2023 vertical jump. Short MAs track the discrete policy steps, while long MAs reveal just two regimes: accommodation versus restriction, with almost no middle ground.

Volatility Contrast: Treasury MA divergence during 2020-2023 exceeded any prior period—the gap between MA3 and MA36 reached 2 percentage points, reflecting genuine market uncertainty. Fed rate MAs show less divergence because changes were deliberate policy steps, not market reactions.

Household Impact: The Treasury MA spread during 2020-2023 measures the unpredictability premium households paid. When 3-month and 36-month moving averages diverge by 2%, long-term financial planning becomes impossible—today’s mortgage rate provides zero information about refinancing options 2-3 years forward. The subsequent reconvergence offers no relief because rates stabilized at elevated levels, not pre-pandemic baselines.
